# Judy Malloy and the Novel With No Front Door

## Before the web made every word clickable, an artist and information programmer built a story you had to search. The result was part fiction, part database, and part argument about who gets to invent a medium.

**By Toolglass | 26 October 2026 | People / software and literature**

On 1 December 1986, readers of the Art Com Electronic Network on The WELL began receiving fragments of a story called *Uncle Roger*. The fragments appeared in an online discussion system, a few at a time, with keywords attached. There was a party in Woodside, California, a narrator called Jenny, and the overheard social weather of Silicon Valley's semiconductor business. It was a novel, but it did not arrive as a book. [1][2]

Its author, Judy Malloy, was not waiting for the World Wide Web. That did not exist as a public medium. Nor was she simply typing a conventional novel into a computer. She was trying to build a different way of telling a story, one in which the reader retrieved fragments and assembled a picture from them. The structure was part of the literature.

The astonishing thing is how many of the ingredients came from jobs that might have looked unrelated to art: library catalogues, technical information systems, database programming, the daily discipline of deciding where a piece of information belongs. Malloy's career makes a nonsense of the familiar division between the person who writes the words and the person who builds the machine.

She did both. Then she made the distinction difficult to maintain.

## The card catalogue before the cursor

Malloy's early career included work as a searcher and editor for the Library of Congress's union catalogue, and as a technical librarian for Ball Aerospace in Colorado. In her own account, she designed and programmed a computer catalogue for the Ball library in 1969. Other work included information organisation for technical and scientific collections. [3]

These are not glamorous origins for a literary revolution. A catalogue is meant to make a collection findable, not to make it sing. Its categories are supposed to be helpful and almost invisible. But information systems have a curious power: they decide which things are neighbours, what a query can retrieve, and which paths through a collection are even possible.

At the same time, Malloy was making visual art and writing. She later described how the logic of information structures gradually entered her studio work. By the late 1970s, she was producing artist's books and card-based narratives. A reader could move through drawings and texts without being forced along a single line. Works including *The Woodpile* (1979) and *Hearst Strip* (1980) explored arrangements that resembled both an artwork and a filing system. [3][4]

It is easy to describe these as primitive hypertext. That is true in one sense and misleading in another. They were physical works with their own visual language, not merely rough drafts for a website. A card catalogue asks you to choose, compare, return and discover. The hand participates in reading. The future digital version inherited that physical intelligence.

Malloy was not the only person experimenting with non-sequential narrative. Readers of Borges and Cortázar, and later users of computer systems such as Ted Nelson's Xanadu or Brown University's hypertext research, would recognise related ambitions. But a shared ambition is not the same as a shared implementation. Malloy came through the studio, the library and the bulletin board. Her route was unusually practical and unusually literary at once.

## The WELL was not the web

The WELL, founded in 1985, was an online community where people used conferencing software to hold discussions. Art Com Electronic Network, associated with curators Carl Loeffler and Fred Truck, made room there for artists working with software, networks and electronic publishing. The medium was text-heavy, social and still small enough that individual contributions could change its atmosphere. [1][5]

In the spring of 1986, Loeffler invited Malloy to work on the network. She began writing *Uncle Roger* that summer. Starting on 1 December, she used an ACEN discussion topic to publish the first file, *A Party in Woodside*, as a serial. The entries were small narrative units, often called *lexias*, accompanied by keywords. Readers could encounter the pieces in a public conversation while the work was still being written. [1][2]

That last detail matters. A contemporary web novel can be posted all at once and then discovered by strangers. *Uncle Roger* emerged in a live social environment. Readers were not merely anonymous future consumers; they were members of a network where the work could be discussed as it unfolded. Malloy's later recollections describe that audience responding to the work. [6]

There is a temptation to call it the first of everything: first hypertext novel, first online novel, first social-media fiction. Historians disagree about boundaries, predecessors and definitions. Toolglass will not manufacture a world-first trophy. What can be said securely is that *Uncle Roger* belongs to the earliest generation of born-digital literary works, and that its networked serial form predates the web by years. The Electronic Literature Directory and the ELMCIP archive document its versions and dates. [7][8]

The actual work was stranger than a milestone.

## The reader as search engine

The first network instalments carried keywords, but the links were not yet the live, clickable web links we take for granted. In 1987 Malloy programmed an interactive version using UNIX shell scripts for ACEN Datanet. She also made an Apple II version in Applesoft BASIC, later developing the larger three-part work and an IBM-compatible BASIC version. A web adaptation arrived in 1995. [1][2][7]

The technology was not an incidental wrapper. Malloy described *Uncle Roger* as a **narrabase**, a narrative database. Instead of turning a page to find out what happened next, the reader searched and retrieved units of the story. Keywords were not just labels on a finished book. They were part of the route by which the story became visible.

In a conventional novel, the author chooses a sequence. Even when a narrator is unreliable or a chronology fractured, the physical book usually presents the reader with a fixed succession of pages. Malloy's system made sequence into an action. A reader following one word could see one set of social connections; another reader, searching differently, could build another impression of the same world.

> **“Remember that at the time, there weren’t models for what I wanted to create.”**
>
> *JUDY MALLOY / THE LITERARY PLATFORM INTERVIEW, 2014 [5]*

The phrase is worth taking seriously. She was not filling a template called 'interactive fiction'. She was designing an interface for a kind of writing that lacked a settled template. There is a difference between authoring content *for* a medium and participating in the invention of the medium itself.

In *Uncle Roger*, the database metaphor even fitted the setting. The story was about the microelectronics culture of 1980s Silicon Valley, where information was valuable, people knew fragments of one another's business, and the machinery of work and power was changing quickly. The reader's own fragmentary access was not merely a trick; it echoed the society being described.

The first file involved a party. The second, *The Blue Notebook*, moved through remembered events and uncertain records. The third, *Terminals*, explored another part of the technological workplace. The Electronic Literature archives describe later versions using Boolean search and generative operations. These are not claims that every version had the same interface. Malloy repeatedly rebuilt the work as the available platforms changed. [2][8][9]

A 1995 web edition gave the project a different kind of reach. It also changed its conditions. A network of artists and technically experienced readers is not the same audience as the public web. Malloy has noted that the authorised web text includes revisions. Preservation here is not a matter of copying a disk image and declaring victory; the work has a history of versions, each with its own relationship between text, code and audience. [7]

## What a story can know

There is a deeper reason this is interesting to programmers. A database makes information retrievable according to a schema, even if the schema is implicit. A search system decides what counts as a match. A link makes one piece of text adjacent to another. Those choices can feel neutral in an office application. In a story, they become decisions about memory, attention and meaning.

Imagine a character appearing in three fragments. In one, they are a friend. In another, a colleague. In a third, a source of suspicion. The order in which a reader encounters those fragments changes the person they imagine. The database has not merely delivered the narrative; it has helped compose the reader's experience of it.

This is not an assertion that the software itself became a human author. Malloy wrote and structured the material. Her artistry lay partly in deciding how the material could be recombined, and how much freedom the reader should have. A work can be interactive without surrendering its authorship to chance.

The question reaches beyond literature. A modern research interface, a museum collection, a personal knowledge base or an AI assistant also mediates between a large body of material and the small selection that appears before us. Every retrieval system tells a partial story. Malloy was unusually early in treating that fact as an artistic resource rather than an inconvenience.

## A machine for bad advice

*Uncle Roger* was not the only oddity in the workshop. Malloy also made *Bad Information*, a project built around dubious advice and the comic possibilities of a searchable collection. People contributed material, and the work developed across installation and network forms. The premise is almost a parody of the solemn authority normally granted to information systems: what if a database were impeccably organised and full of terrible guidance? [3][10]

The joke has aged alarmingly well. Searchability does not confer truth. An answer can be retrieved quickly, formatted beautifully and still be nonsense. This is a lesson that modern AI products have spent a great deal of money rediscovering.

But *Bad Information* was not simply an early version of a misinformation warning. It was an artwork that used the social mechanics of collecting, categorising and sharing material. Its form made the audience part of the system. That is the recurring Malloy move: an information structure becomes a cultural structure.

There were other experiments. *YOU!* (1991) invited multiple contributors into an online poem. *its name was Penelope*, published by Eastgate in the early 1990s, used a different narrative architecture. The long poem *The Roar of Destiny Emanated From the Refrigerator* sounds like a title written by someone who knows perfectly well how absurd the technological sublime can become. Malloy's work did not settle into a single product or format. [3][11]

## Two writers, a research laboratory, and email

One of the most revealing later works is *Forward Anywhere*, made with researcher Cathy Marshall in the 1990s through the artist-in-residence programme at Xerox PARC. Their raw material included email exchanges about their lives and memories. Instead of starting with a fixed plot and illustrating it, the collaborators allowed resonances between their accounts to shape the hypertext. [12]

This is a useful reminder that the history of software is not only a parade of processors, languages and famous corporate laboratories. It is also a history of people asking what a medium might permit them to express. PARC is remembered for graphical interfaces, networking and the machinery of the modern office. Malloy and Marshall belong in that picture too, because they were exploring what those tools could mean for writing and human experience.

Malloy went on to document and advocate for other artists working at the intersection of technology and culture. She edited *Women, Art, and Technology* (MIT Press, 2003) and *Social Media Archeology and Poetics* (2016), and worked on Arts Wire and Leonardo-related publications. A School of the Art Institute of Chicago faculty profile lists her teaching *Women Artists in Cyberspace* in spring 2026. [3][11][13]

This is not a career of one early invention followed by a museum label. It is a long argument, made in artworks, code, teaching and editorial labour, that creative people should have a hand in shaping the tools through which culture moves.

> **She did not put a novel on a computer. She made the computer part of what a novel could be.**
>
> *TOOLGLASS / EDITORIAL EXTRACT 02*

## A front door is not always necessary

Today a great deal of digital writing arrives through systems designed to hide their workings. A person enters a prompt, a service produces a passage, and the apparatus in between is mostly inaccessible. This can be useful, but it makes it easy to mistake convenience for authorship and output for design.

Malloy's work points in another direction. She had a literary idea, then built and rebuilt the mechanisms that allowed it to exist. She treated a catalogue, a query, a keyword and a screen as things an artist could think with. Her software was not a black box that produced the story for her. It was an instrument she shaped to make the story possible.

The first readers of *Uncle Roger* did not need to understand every UNIX shell script to participate. They did, however, encounter a work whose structure was unmistakably part of its meaning. The interface did not disappear. It invited curiosity.

Forty years after those first postings on The WELL, we have an astonishing surplus of software that promises to make writing effortless. It is worth remembering a writer who chose the more difficult path: to invent a kind of writing and then make the computer learn how to hold it.

A book usually offers a front cover, a first page and a direction of travel. Malloy built a place where you could enter by a keyword, find a stranger at a party, follow a fragment of memory and come out with a story that belonged partly to the path you had taken. The novel had no single front door. That was the point.

### Toolbox / things a reader might want to look up

**The WELL:** A pioneering online community founded in 1985, known for its discussion forums. **ACEN:** Art Com Electronic Network, an early venue for electronic art and writing on The WELL. **BBS:** Bulletin board system, a networked place for messages and files before the public web. **Lexia:** A small unit of text used in hypertext writing; it may link to or be retrieved alongside others. **Narrabase:** Malloy's term for a narrative database in which searching and retrieval shape the reading experience. **Applesoft BASIC:** A BASIC interpreter for the Apple II, used by Malloy for a disk version of *Uncle Roger*. **UNIX shell scripts:** Programs made from command-line instructions; the 1987 online interactive version used them. **Xerox PARC:** A research laboratory whose artist-in-residence programme supported Malloy and Cathy Marshall's collaboration. **Eastgate:** A publisher associated with early literary hypertext.
